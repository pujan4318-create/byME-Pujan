GEO and RAG Explained: How AI Search Decides Which Pages to Cite

By Pujan, November 6, 2026

GEO is the practice of shaping your content so AI systems choose it as a source and cite it. RAG is the technique many of them use to find that source. Here is how both work in plain language, and what I would change on a page first.

I learned SEO the usual way: keywords, title tags, backlinks, and a lot of time in Search Console. Then my own search habits changed. I started asking ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google's AI answers my questions and getting one written answer instead of ten blue links. That raised a question I could not ignore: where do those answers come from, and why does one website get named while another, sometimes ranking higher, gets skipped? Over the past months I have researched GEO and RAG to answer it.

The short answer

GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) is the practice of shaping your content so AI systems choose it as a source and cite it inside their answers.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is the technique many AI tools use to answer from fresh documents: they first retrieve relevant text, then generate an answer using that text.

RAG is the mechanism. GEO is what you do, as a website owner, to work with that mechanism.

What is GEO?

The term comes from a research paper, "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization", by researchers from Princeton University, Georgia Tech, the Allen Institute for AI, and IIT Delhi. It was presented at the KDD 2024 conference. The team built a benchmark of 10,000 queries, called GEO-bench, and tested nine ways of rewriting content to see which made a source more visible in generative answers.

What the research found

Some content changes raised a source's visibility by up to 40% in the benchmark. The changes that worked best were simple: adding quotations from relevant sources, adding statistics, and citing sources. The team also tested the ideas on Perplexity, a real generative engine, and reported gains there as well.

Two cautions. First, "up to 40%" is the best case in an experiment, not a promise for your site. Second, the benchmark tested content changes, not a full real-world ranking system. I treat it as strong evidence about direction, not a guarantee.

What is RAG?

A language model on its own only knows what was in its training data. That data has a cutoff, and the model can state wrong things confidently. Retrieval-Augmented Generation was introduced in a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues, presented at NeurIPS 2020, as a way to ground answers in retrieved documents instead of memory alone.

How RAG works, step by step

  1. The question comes in. The system works out what you are really asking and may rewrite it into one or more search queries.
  2. Retrieval. It searches an index of documents, often a web search or a vector database, and pulls back the most relevant passages. Pages are usually split into smaller pieces called chunks, so the system retrieves a passage rather than a whole page.
  3. Augmentation. The retrieved passages are added to the prompt the model sees, along with your question.
  4. Generation. The model writes an answer using those passages, and often shows which sources it used.

ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and Google's AI features do not publish their exact pipelines. This is the general pattern, not a blueprint of any one product.

How GEO and RAG connect

Once you see RAG as a pipeline, GEO is easier to reason about. Your page can fail at two points.

  • It never gets retrieved. The crawler cannot reach it, the text only appears after JavaScript runs, or the headings are so vague that no chunk clearly matches the question.
  • It gets retrieved but not used. The passage is fluffy, makes claims with no evidence, or buries the answer, so the model leans on a clearer source.

GEO is mostly about fixing both. That is also why ordinary SEO has not become irrelevant. Google's guidance on AI features and your website says SEO best practices remain relevant, and its guide to optimizing for generative AI features stresses creating unique, useful content. If a page is not crawlable and indexable, it has little chance of being retrieved at all.

What this means for your pages

  1. Put the answer first. Under each heading, answer the question in the first sentence or two, then explain.
  2. One idea per section. Systems retrieve chunks, so a section that mixes three topics matches none of them well.
  3. Use specific numbers and name their source. A figure with a named source is something a model can safely repeat.
  4. Cite your sources. Link to the original study or documentation.
  5. Make sure the text is in the HTML. View the page source and search for a sentence from your article. If it is missing, some crawlers may never see it.
  6. Check crawler access. Look at your robots.txt and security plugin settings. Each AI company documents its crawlers, so check their current documentation rather than copying a list from a blog post.
  7. Keep your SEO basics healthy. Titles, internal links, speed, and indexing still decide whether you are in the pool of pages that can be retrieved.

How you can test this yourself

  1. Pick 10 questions your audience really asks, including some Nepal-specific ones.
  2. Ask each in ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity, and write down which sites each one cites.
  3. Rewrite one of your pages using the changes above.
  4. Wait two to four weeks, ask the same questions again, and compare.

Keep honest notes, including when nothing changes. A single test proves little, because results differ between tools and between days.

What I still do not know

I do not know exactly how each AI product chooses its sources, and nobody outside those companies does. The research behind GEO is young, so some of today's advice may not hold up. If a guide promises guaranteed AI citations, be skeptical.

Frequently asked questions

Is GEO replacing SEO?

No. GEO builds on SEO. Your page still has to be crawlable, indexed, and useful before an AI system can retrieve it.

Do I need to code to understand RAG?

No. You only need the idea: the system retrieves text first and writes the answer second.

Can I guarantee my site will be cited by ChatGPT?

No one can. You can make your content easier to retrieve and safer to quote, but the AI system makes the final choice.
